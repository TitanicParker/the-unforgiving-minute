The Unforgiving
Minute.

The freedom to love

A reflectionApproximately 8 minutes spoken
A moment to consider

“Love your enemies.”

Reading size

Jesus said something extraordinary.

He said, "Love your enemies."

Now, when we hear those words, we tend to think Jesus is asking us to do something terribly difficult. Almost impossible.

We imagine someone who has offended us, hurt us, angered us, or treated us unfairly. And we imagine that Jesus is telling us to somehow manufacture a feeling of love towards that person.

But what if we've misunderstood the instruction?

What if Jesus wasn't simply telling us to change how we feel about another person?

What if he was pointing us towards something much more immediate?

Something happening inside ourselves.

Something we have the opportunity to notice every single day.

And something which, if we understand it, could transform the way we experience our lives.

Let me give you a simple example.

There's a man called John.

John is sitting quietly at home. He's comfortable. He's peaceful. He's enjoying his own company.

And then Mary walks into the room.

Mary asks him a question.

Perhaps it's an innocent question. Perhaps her timing isn't ideal. Perhaps there's something in her tone.

Whatever it is, John feels irritated.

And almost instantly, he thinks, "There she goes again. I was perfectly happy until she came in."

He turns to Mary and says, "Why can't you leave me alone? I was fine until you walked in here and upset me."

Now Mary is upset.

She says, "I only asked you a question! There's no need to speak to me like that."

And suddenly we've got two unhappy people.

Two people who, only moments earlier, weren't arguing at all.

And each of them believes the other person is responsible for the way they feel.

John believes Mary has upset him.

Mary believes John has upset her.

And so the conflict begins.

But let's look a little more closely.

Because something very important has happened here.

Mary walked into the room. She said something. John experienced a feeling.

And between what Mary said and what John experienced, there was an interpretation.

John didn't simply hear Mary's words.

He interpreted them.

He evaluated them.

He gave them meaning.

And that meaning was shaped by his expectations, his conditioning, his beliefs and his state of mind.

But John didn't notice any of that.

He experienced the whole thing as one seamless event.

Mary came in. Mary upset me.

That's his reality.

And because it feels so obviously true, he never thinks to question it.

But here's the extraordinary possibility.

What if John could catch himself in that moment?

What if, just as the irritation arose, instead of turning towards Mary with an accusation, he turned his attention towards himself?

And asked one simple question:

“

What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?

The question that changes everything

Think about that question.

Not, "Why is Mary upsetting me?"

Not, "Why does she always do this?"

Not, "How can I make her understand?"

But, "What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?"

Perhaps John discovers that he believes Mary shouldn't interrupt him.

Perhaps he believes that because he's peaceful, everyone else has an obligation to preserve his peace.

Perhaps he believes that another person has the power to take his peace away.

And suddenly he sees something he hadn't seen before.

He has placed a condition upon his own happiness.

He can be peaceful, but only if Mary behaves the way he wants her to behave.

And Mary doesn't even know that this condition exists!

Now, here's the remarkable thing.

John doesn't necessarily have to replace that belief with another belief.

He doesn't have to repeat positive affirmations.

He doesn't have to force himself to be calm.

He simply has to see clearly what he's been believing.

And sometimes, when we see a mistaken belief clearly enough, it begins to fall away.

Not because we've fought it.

But because we've recognised it.

And that recognition changes everything.

Because anger is not necessarily our state of being.

Anger is a state of mind. Anger is a filter.

And when we're angry, we look through that filter and mistake what we see for reality itself.

John sees Mary through the filter of his irritation.

And the more he looks through it, the more justified his irritation appears.

But if he can notice the filter, he doesn't have to obey it.

He can feel the anger without becoming its servant.

And this brings us back to Jesus.

"Love your enemies."

Perhaps the first enemy we need to recognise isn't the person standing in front of us.

Perhaps it's the hostile interpretation arising within us that has made that person appear to be our enemy.

And perhaps loving our enemy begins by softening our resistance to what we're experiencing inside ourselves.

Not suppressing it.

Not condemning ourselves for it.

Not pretending it isn't there.

But recognising it.

And refusing to turn it automatically into conflict.

Now, there's a very important element to all of this.

Time.

Because John doesn't have half an hour to sit down and analyse his conditioning.

Mary is standing there.

She's waiting for an answer.

The feeling has arisen.

The words are almost on his lips.

And he has a choice.

He can follow the familiar reaction.

Or he can take that moment to become conscious of what's happening.

The poet Rudyard Kipling wrote about filling the unforgiving minute with sixty seconds' worth of distance run.

I think there's something beautiful about that expression.

The unforgiving minute.

Because life doesn't wait until we're ready.

It presents us with the moment.

And asks what we're going to do with it.

John has perhaps three seconds.

Three seconds in which he can repeat an old pattern, or begin to break it.

Three seconds in which he can blame Mary, or investigate his own experience.

Three seconds in which he can turn a perfectly ordinary encounter into an argument, or into an opportunity for understanding.

And that is where courage comes in.

Because it takes courage to question your own reaction when every fibre of your being is telling you that you're right.

It takes courage to recognise that the anger you're feeling is real, but the story you're telling yourself about its cause may not be the whole truth.

And it takes determination to do that in the very moment when your conditioning is urging you to do the opposite.

But notice something else.

John isn't doing this for Mary's sake.

At least, not primarily.

He's doing it for himself.

Because he's the one experiencing the anger.

He's the one whose peace has been disturbed.

He's the one being invited to discover a different relationship with his own experience.

And if he can do that, Mary benefits naturally.

He doesn't need to make a great moral effort to be nice to her.

He may simply find that he no longer needs to attack her.

Now, of course, other people can genuinely hurt us. They can behave cruelly, threaten us, or violate our boundaries. Loving them doesn't mean accepting mistreatment, and our first emotional reactions are not always voluntary.

But even then, there's a distinction between what happens to us and what we do with what arises within us.

And that's where our freedom begins.

Jesus also asked why we notice the speck in our brother's eye while overlooking the plank in our own.

The oxygen mask principle

Think of the instruction we hear before an aeroplane takes off: put on your own oxygen mask before helping someone else with theirs.

It isn't selfishness. It's the recognition that if you cannot breathe, you may be unable to help the person beside you.

And there is a striking parallel in our relationships.

Before John tries to correct Mary's behaviour, he needs to notice what is happening within himself. Before he can meet her with understanding, he needs to recognise the anger and the assumptions through which he is seeing her.

See clearly within yourself before trying to correct what you see in another.

This doesn't mean John must become perfect before he can help Mary. It means that attending to his own state first may make genuine help—and genuine love—possible.

Isn't that precisely what's happening with John?

He's intensely aware of Mary's supposed wrongdoing.

But almost completely unaware of the interpretation he's making.

He sees Mary.

But he doesn't see himself seeing Mary.

And perhaps that's the deeper invitation.

To become conscious of our own participation in the reality we're experiencing.

Because we spend so much of our lives trying to arrange the world so that we can feel the way we want to feel.

We want other people to agree with us.

To appreciate us.

To understand us.

To leave us alone when we want peace.

To behave according to our expectations.

And when they don't, we suffer.

But perhaps the answer isn't always to rearrange the world.

Perhaps sometimes the answer is to recognise the conditions we've placed upon our own peace.

And to discover that we have more freedom than we imagined.

Think of how much human conflict begins with two people who are each convinced that the other person has caused their unhappiness.

Two people reacting to their own interpretations.

Two people defending their own feelings.

Two people who have forgotten that the person standing opposite them is another human being, with their own fears, conditioning, hopes and vulnerabilities.

And Jesus says, "Love one another."

Not because everybody will agree with you.

Not because nobody will ever offend you.

Not because the world will arrange itself around your preferences.

But because love is possible even when those conditions are not met.

And perhaps love is not something we must struggle to manufacture.

Perhaps it's something that becomes available when we stop defending every hostile thought that passes through our minds.

When we recognise anger as a passing filter, rather than the truth of who we are.

When we allow another person to be different without automatically making them an enemy.

And when we take responsibility for the part we play in our own experience.

So the next time someone walks into your room, interrupts your peace, says something you don't like, or challenges your understanding of the world, remember John and Mary.

Remember the unforgiving minute.

And before you speak, if you possibly can, ask yourself:

"What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?"

You may discover something about yourself that you never knew.

You may discover that the other person isn't the enemy you imagined.

And you may discover that the peace you thought somebody had taken away was never entirely theirs to give or take.

Because the great challenge of being human is not simply to know what is right.

It is to become capable of living it.

Not tomorrow.

Not when everybody else has changed.

But here.

In this moment.

In the unforgiving minute.

And perhaps that is where loving your enemy truly begins.

Amen.

Preparing the second reading…

The question that changes the encounter

What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?

This is not a demand to stop feeling. It is a way to notice the interpretation that can turn a passing feeling into a grievance. The question does not establish that the other person is innocent, nor that every emotion is chosen. It opens a little space between the feeling and the story we tell about it.

Each example below offers two ways the same everyday encounter might unfold. The second is not a script to memorise. It shows how an honest inward question can make a more creative response possible.

✦

1. The interruption “You never leave me alone.”

The familiar reaction

Mary: “John, have you thought about what we're doing tomorrow?”

John: “For God's sake, can't you see I'm reading? Can't I have five minutes to myself?”

Mary: “I only asked a question. There's no need to bite my head off.”

John: “You never give me any peace.”

The inward turn

John feels irritation. Before he speaks, he asks: “What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?”

Perhaps: Mary should know not to interrupt me. If she cared about my peace, she would leave me alone. Her question is an intrusion.

He sees that his wish for quiet is reasonable, but his interpretation of Mary's intention is not a fact.

A different response

John: “I'm really enjoying this chapter. Could we talk about tomorrow in twenty minutes?”

Mary: “Of course.”

For practice: Can I ask for quiet without making the other person wrong for speaking?

✦

2. The forgotten bins “Nothing I do is ever good enough.”

The familiar reaction

Mary: “Did you put the bins out?”

John: “I forgot.”

Mary: “That's the third time this month.”

John: “Here we go. Nothing I do is ever good enough for you.”

Mary: “I was talking about the bins, John.”

The inward turn

John notices defensiveness and asks: “What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?”

Perhaps: If I forget something, I'm inadequate. Mary's reminder is a judgment on my worth. I have to defend myself.

The bins are a practical matter; his worth is not on trial.

A different response

John: “You're right. I forgot again. I'll set a reminder.”

Mary: “Thanks.”

For practice: Can I acknowledge an error without turning it into a verdict on who I am?

✦

3. The phone “You don't care about me.”

The familiar reaction

Mary is telling John about a difficult day. John looks at his phone.

Mary: “You're not listening. You don't care what I have to say.”

John: “Don't start interrogating me.”

Mary: “You never listen.”

The inward turn

This time Mary asks: “What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?”

Perhaps: If John looks away, I don't matter. His distraction means he doesn't love me.

She can recognise the assumption without denying that she wants his attention.

A different response

Mary: “This matters to me. Could you put the phone down for a few minutes and listen?”

John: “Sorry, I was distracted. Go on.”

For practice: Can I ask clearly for what I need without making the other person's behaviour proof that I am unloved?

✦

4. Running late “You have no respect for my time.”

The familiar reaction

John: “You're late again. You have absolutely no respect for my time.”

Mary: “I'm nearly ready.”

John: “That's what you said last Christmas!”

Soon they are arguing about years of grievances, not tonight's reservation.

The inward turn

John asks: “What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?”

Perhaps: Mary is late because she doesn't respect me. Every previous delay proves it.

He still wants to arrive on time. But he no longer has to treat the delay as a personal insult.

A different response

John: “We need to leave in five minutes to make the booking. How much longer do you need?”

Mary: “Ten minutes.”

John: “I'll ring ahead.”

For practice: Can I address today's problem without recruiting every old grievance to support it?

✦

5. The harsh accusation “How dare you speak to me like that?”

The familiar reaction

Mary: “You're selfish. You never think of anyone but yourself.”

John: “How dare you? You're the selfish one!”

The inward turn

John feels anger and asks: “What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?”

Perhaps: Mary's words define me. If I don't retaliate, I'm agreeing with her. My dignity depends on winning.

Mary's words may be unfair or hurtful. Awareness does not require John to accept abuse.

A different response

John: “I hear that you're angry. I'm willing to talk about what happened, but not while we're calling each other names.”

For practice: Can I protect a boundary without trying to wound the person who crossed it?

✦

6. The birthday “If you loved me, you'd have known.”

The familiar reaction

Mary: “I thought you might have planned something special.”

John: “I bought you flowers.”

Mary: “You should know what matters to me by now.”

John: “Nothing is ever enough.”

The inward turn

Mary asks: “What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?”

Perhaps: If John really loved me, he'd anticipate my wishes. Having to ask makes me less important.

Her disappointment is real. Her conclusion about his love is an interpretation.

A different response

Mary: “Thank you for the flowers. I was hoping we'd spend a special evening together. Could we arrange something?”

John: “Yes. I'd like that.”

For practice: Can I express a wish without turning an unmet expectation into an accusation?

✦

The practice: the unforgiving minute

1. Notice the heat. Anger, hurt, fear or defensiveness has arisen. You do not have to pretend otherwise. 2. Ask the question. What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way? 3. Listen for the hidden premise. “They should…”, “This means…”, “If they loved me…”, “I must…”. 4. Separate fact from interpretation. What was said or done? What meaning did I add? 5. Respond creatively. Ask, explain, apologise, set a boundary, or request a moment. You need not obey the first impulse.

Sometimes the belief loosens as soon as it is seen. Sometimes it doesn't. The practice is not a test of spiritual success, and not every conflict is a misunderstanding. Other people's conduct can genuinely harm us; safety and accountability still matter.

The opportunity is simpler: to feel what arises without automatically accepting the first story it tells.

The unforgiving minute is not the time in which we must become perfect. It is the time in which we may become aware.

And perhaps that is where loving your enemy begins.
