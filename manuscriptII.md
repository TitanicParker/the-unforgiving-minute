# The Second Question
*From Awareness to Compassion — A Continuation of The Unforgiving Minute*

There comes a point in the development of a human being when something remarkable becomes possible.

We become available to another person.

Not merely available to speak to them, listen to them, or share a room with them.

But genuinely available to understand them.

And that is a much greater achievement than it might first appear.

Because, for much of our lives, when we encounter another person, we are not simply encountering them.

We are encountering our own reactions to them.

Our expectations. Our memories. Our conditioning. Our assumptions about how people should behave.

And all of these things can stand between us and the person who is actually there.

In *The Unforgiving Minute*, we considered a simple question.

**What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?**

We saw how John, sitting peacefully at home, could become irritated when Mary entered the room and asked him a question.

We saw how easily he could attribute his disturbance to Mary.

And we saw that, by examining the belief underlying his reaction, John could discover a freedom he hadn't previously recognised.

But that was only the beginning.

Because there is a second question.

And the second question opens an entirely different possibility.

## The work has a purpose

Let's consider something important.

When John first decides to examine his reactions, he is undertaking a deliberate piece of work.

He has recognised that his habitual responses are interfering with the quality of his life.

He doesn't like becoming angry.

He doesn't enjoy conflict.

He doesn't want to spend his days defending himself against people who, in many cases, have done little more than behave differently from how he expected.

So he makes a decision.

He is going to approach these encounters differently.

And whenever he notices that familiar heat of irritation, resentment, defensiveness or hurt, he will ask himself:

*What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?*

At first, this may be difficult.

The reaction is quick.

The impulse to speak is immediate.

Mary is standing there, waiting for an answer.

And John must remember to interrupt a habit that may have been operating for decades.

This takes courage.

It takes determination.

And it takes practice.

But something happens as John continues.

He begins to recognise familiar beliefs.

He sees the assumptions that have repeatedly governed his experience.

He discovers that the feeling of being offended does not necessarily mean that an offence has occurred.

He discovers that another person's disagreement need not threaten his worth.

He discovers that he can experience anger without accepting the interpretation that anger offers him.

And gradually, the work changes.

What once required deliberate effort begins to happen naturally.

The question becomes familiar.

The mechanism becomes recognisable.

And John finds himself increasingly available to thoughts that would once have been obscured by his reactions.

**He is not merely learning to control his anger. He is becoming less dependent upon the beliefs that previously gave rise to it.**

That is a very different achievement.

## The work is finite

Now, this deserves particular attention.

We sometimes speak about self-awareness as though it were an endless project.

As though the human being must spend an entire lifetime examining, correcting and repairing themselves.

But why should that be so?

When we undertake to learn something, we expect to become better at it.

We learn to read.

We learn to swim.

We learn to play an instrument.

And although there may always be more to discover, we eventually acquire a competence that no longer requires the effort of a beginner.

Why should self-awareness be entirely different?

John does not need to discover every possible belief he might ever hold.

He needs to become familiar with the way belief operates in his experience.

He needs to recognise how a thought becomes an interpretation, how an interpretation acquires the appearance of reality, and how that apparent reality can provoke an emotional response.

Once he understands this mechanism, he can recognise it in situations he has never previously encountered.

And as that recognition becomes established, his relationship with his own mind changes.

This does not mean John becomes incapable of anger.

It does not mean he becomes perfect.

It does not mean he ceases to be human.

It means that he has developed and maintained a human capacity.

**The work of establishing that capacity can reach maturity, even though the opportunities to exercise it continue throughout life.**

And that matters enormously.

Because the purpose of the work was never to remain occupied with the work.

The purpose was freedom.

And freedom is valuable because of what it makes possible.

## The moment after the moment

So let us return to John and Mary.

Mary enters the room.

She asks John why he hasn't done something he promised to do.

John feels a momentary irritation.

But now he recognises it.

He notices the assumption that Mary's question is an accusation against his character.

He sees that he has interpreted her words as a threat to his worth.

And because he recognises the interpretation, he doesn't have to defend it.

The heat begins to soften.

Perhaps the entire process takes only a second.

Perhaps he hardly needs to put the question into words anymore.

He has become familiar with himself.

But now what?

Mary is still standing there.

She has still asked her question.

And John, no longer preoccupied with defending himself, finds that something new is available.

He can become interested in Mary.

Not in what Mary has done to him.

Not in whether Mary is right or wrong.

But in what Mary is experiencing.

And now he can ask the second question.

**What might Mary need to believe is true in order to feel the way she does?**

Consider the difference.

The first question directed John's attention towards his own interpretation.

The second directs his attention towards Mary's.

The first allowed him to recognise his conditioning.

The second invites him to appreciate that Mary, too, is living through an understanding of reality shaped by her own experiences, expectations and beliefs.

And suddenly the encounter has changed.

John is no longer defending himself against Mary.

He is trying to understand her.

## The world as Mary sees it

Perhaps Mary believes John has forgotten something important to her.

Perhaps she believes that when he makes a promise and doesn't keep it, she cannot rely upon him.

Perhaps she feels that she is carrying more responsibility than he is.

Perhaps she is worried about something John knows nothing about.

Or perhaps she has simply had a difficult day.

John cannot know any of this merely by examining his own mind.

And that is important.

**The second question is not an invitation to decide what Mary believes. It is an invitation to become curious enough to find out.**

So John might say:

"Mary, is this important to you because you feel you can't rely on me when I say I'll do something?"

And Mary might answer:

"Yes, John. That's exactly it."

Or she might say:

"No. I'm worried because we need it done by tomorrow."

Either way, John has learned something.

He has allowed Mary to explain her own experience rather than insisting upon the explanation his first reaction supplied.

And Mary, perhaps for the first time in that encounter, feels that John is genuinely interested in understanding her.

There may still be a practical problem to resolve.

There may still be a promise to keep.

There may still be an apology to offer.

But the encounter is no longer organised around two people defending themselves against one another.

It has become a conversation.

And that is no small thing.

## Put on your own oxygen mask first

Remember the instruction given to passengers before an aeroplane takes off.

Put on your own oxygen mask before attempting to help someone else.

We understand the wisdom of that immediately.

If you cannot breathe, you may be unable to help the person beside you.

But notice something.

**The instruction does not end with putting on your own oxygen mask.**

That is only the first step.

You put it on so that you are capable of helping.

And perhaps this is a beautiful way to understand the teaching of Jesus about the speck in another person's eye and the plank in our own.

We are invited to attend to our own vision first.

Not because the other person is unimportant.

Not because we should become endlessly preoccupied with ourselves.

But because our own unexamined perceptions may prevent us from seeing the other person clearly.

And once we have attended to our own vision, we become available to help.

The first question removes an obstacle.

The second creates a bridge.

## Love becomes possible

Jesus said, "Love your enemies."

We have already considered how difficult that instruction appears when we imagine it as a command to replace hatred with affection.

But perhaps we can now see another dimension.

When John is angry with Mary, he experiences her through the filter of his anger.

She appears unreasonable.

She appears inconsiderate.

She may even appear deliberately hostile.

But when John recognises his own interpretation, he is no longer compelled to see her in that way.

And when he asks what Mary might need to believe in order to feel as she does, something further happens.

He begins to encounter her humanity.

He recognises that she, too, has an inner world.

She, too, experiences feelings that seem to arise directly from the behaviour of others.

She, too, may mistake her interpretations for reality.

She, too, is doing her best to navigate a life she does not completely understand.

And now the apparent distance between them begins to diminish.

John does not need Mary to become identical to him.

He does not need her to agree with him.

He does not need her to be free of her own conditioning.

He can simply recognise that the processes operating within Mary are not fundamentally foreign to those operating within himself.

**The person he was preparing to oppose is another human being experiencing life from a different point of view.**

And that recognition may be the beginning of compassion.

Not compassion manufactured through moral effort.

But compassion arising naturally from understanding.

## The creative possibility

And now we come to something even more significant.

Human life is creative.

We are not confined to repeating the same responses to the same circumstances.

We can learn.

We can develop.

We can establish new capacities.

And those capacities can change the quality of the life available to us.

John's original reaction offered him a narrow range of possibilities.

He could defend himself.

He could accuse Mary.

He could withdraw.

He could suppress his anger and pretend nothing was wrong.

But once he recognised his reaction, and once he became curious about Mary's experience, an entirely different range of possibilities opened.

He could listen.

He could ask.

He could apologise.

He could laugh.

He could explain himself without hostility.

He could recognise something important that he had previously overlooked.

He could even discover that Mary was right.

And none of these possibilities required him to diminish himself.

Quite the opposite.

They became available because he was no longer occupied with protecting an image of himself.

**His freedom from the old reaction had become freedom for a new relationship.**

That is the creative achievement.

Not simply avoiding conflict.

But becoming capable of something that previously seemed unavailable.

## From self-awareness to fellowship

There is a danger in making self-awareness the ultimate objective of human development.

We could become so concerned with examining ourselves that we forget why we began.

We could become peaceful but indifferent.

Self-contained but disconnected.

Highly conscious of our own experience, yet uninterested in the experience of anyone else.

But surely that is not the destination.

We do not live alone.

We live among other human beings.

We love, work, disagree, cooperate, misunderstand, forgive and begin again.

And the quality of those relationships is inseparable from the quality of our lives.

So perhaps the purpose of cultivating self-awareness is not simply to become more comfortable within ourselves.

Perhaps it is to become more available to one another.

And that gives us a beautiful progression.

We begin by recognising our own reactions.

We learn to question the beliefs that give them their authority.

We practise until this recognition becomes an established capacity.

And then, with less of our attention occupied by self-defence, we discover that we have something wonderful to offer another person.

Our attention.

Our curiosity.

Our willingness to understand.

Our love.

Not because we have been commanded to manufacture these things.

But because we have created the inner freedom in which they can flourish.

## The second question

So the next time somebody says something that disturbs you, remember the first question.

**What would I need to believe is true in order to feel this way?**

Give yourself the opportunity to recognise what is happening within you.

And when the reaction begins to loosen, when you find that you no longer need to defend the story it has created, remember that there is another question waiting.

**What might this person need to believe is true in order to feel the way they do?**

And then listen.

Not to correct.

Not to diagnose.

Not to win.

But to understand.

Because the person standing before you is not simply an event occurring in your life.

They are another life.

Another centre of experience.

Another human being attempting to make sense of existence.

And perhaps the greatest fruit of self-awareness is that we can finally begin to meet one another without so much of ourselves standing in the way.

We put on our own oxygen mask.

And then we turn towards the person beside us.

We attend to the plank in our own eye.

And then we begin to see our neighbour more clearly.

We discover freedom within ourselves.

And then we discover what that freedom is for.

**The first question teaches us how to stop making enemies.**

**The second teaches us how to begin understanding friends.**

And somewhere in that movement, love ceases to be merely an instruction.

It becomes an available way of being.

Not beyond our humanity.

But more fully within it.

And perhaps that is what Jesus was inviting us to discover all along.

Amen.
